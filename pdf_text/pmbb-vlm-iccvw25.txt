Abstract
Recent advances in vision–language models (VLMs)
have enabled strong medical image understanding, yet most
remain limited to 2D inputs and scale poorly to 3D CT and
MRI data. Directly applying 3D encoders is computation-
ally costly and often weakens diagnostic signals by includ-
ing irrelevant slices. We propose a generalizable framework
to adapt 2D medical VLMs to 3D imaging through princi-
pled slice selection and multimodal instruction tuning. Our
two-stage pipeline first aligns image–text pairs with caption
supervision, then fine-tunes on synthetic diagnostic conver-
sations grounded in radiology reports. Using PMBB data,
we benchmark energy-based and K-Center strategies across
accuracy, explanation quality, and efficiency. Results show
that selecting about half of the slices (e.g., K=5 of 11)
yields the best diagnostic accuracy while reducing train-
ing cost by over 40%. Explanation quality, however, does
not always track accuracy. Coverage and overlap analy-
ses indicate strong complementarity across strategies, sug-
gesting that diverse slice selection can recover most cor-
rect cases with compact subsets. These findings highlight
the importance of visual–textual pretraining, task-specific
fine-tuning, and principled slice selection in scaling medi-
cal VLMs to volumetric data.
1. Introduction
Recent progress in vision–language modeling has enabled
significant advances in both general and biomedical AI.

Modern vision–language models (VLMs) align visual and
textual signals for captioning, VQA, and instruction follow-
ing [1, 5, 8, 13, 17, 19–21, 25, 29, 31, 34]. These foun-
dation models have been adapted to medicine by domain-
specific systems such as LLaVA-Med, Med-Flamingo, and
RadFM, which extend pretrained VLMs to disease reason-
ing and report-grounded interaction [18, 24, 32].
Yet most VLMs are architected for 2D inputs and strug-
gle with volumetric CT/MRI, where inter-slice context and
anatomical continuity are essential. Directly adopting na-
tive 3D architectures (e.g., 3D U-Net/nnU-Net/Swin-3D-
style transformers) improves volumetric context but signifi-
cantly raises compute and can inject noise from irrelevant
regions [7, 14, 26].
Recent 3D transformer designs and
large-scale medical pretraining further underscore this ac-
curacy–efficiency tension [4, 11, 22, 38, 39]. Meanwhile,
vision–language approaches that bridge text with 3D vol-
umes (e.g., CT-CLIP) highlight the promise of leveraging
language priors without fully 3D encoders [10].
We address this gap with a general, architecture-
preserving framework that adapts 2D VLMs to 3D un-
derstanding via principled slice selection and lightweight
multi-slice token fusion. A 3D scan is decomposed into
2D axial slices; an informative subset is chosen using en-
ergy ranking or k-center sampling [12, 16, 23], encoded by
a shared vision backbone, and concatenated into a single
visual token sequence for the VLM. This compact-subset
view is consistent with recent evidence that (i) carefully
choosing a few slices can rival full-volume processing and
(ii) slice-level cues/prompting can steer VLM attention to-
ward diagnostically salient anatomy [30]. From the data
side, annotation-efficient studies suggest allocating fewer

Training follows a two-stage recipe: Stage I aligns im-
ages with figure-caption supervision to ground the visual
encoder; Stage II instruction-tunes the model with diag-
nostic dialogues synthesized from PMBB reports. This de-
coupled pretraining–tuning paradigm mirrors successful de-
signs in general VLMs while remaining parameter-efficient
for multi-slice inputs. We evaluate along three axes: diag-
nosis accuracy, explanation quality, and computational ef-
ficiency ,and find that a moderate subset size (about half
the volume, e.g., K=5 of 11) offers the best trade-off;
explanation quality and accuracy are not strictly coupled;
efficient selection reduces training time by >40% versus
whole-volume; and both vision–language pretraining and
task-specific fine-tuning are essential.
Our contributions are threefold:
• A scalable framework that adapts 2D medical VLMs to
volumetric scans through principled slice selection and
lightweight multi-slice token integration.
• A two-stage pipeline demonstrating that visual–textual
pretraining followed by domain-specific fine-tuning is
crucial for accurate and interpretable medical reasoning.
• A compact yet comprehensive evaluation protocol span-
ning balanced and general accuracy, both individual
and intersection explanation metrics, and coverage/over-
lap/error analyses, with practical guidance on slice count
(around half), strategy choice (e.g., k-Center, energy),
and efficiency–accuracy trade-offs for deployment.
2. Related Work
Vision–Language Models for Medical AI. Foundational
vision–language models (VLMs) learn joint visual–textual
representations through contrastive/generative pretraining
and, increasingly, instruction tuning [1, 5, 8, 13, 17, 19–
21, 25, 29, 31, 34].
These capabilities have been trans-
ferred to clinical settings by domain-adapted systems such
as LLaVA-Med, Med-Flamingo, and RadFM, as well as
broader biomedical efforts like BiomedGPT, PMC-VQA,
and generalist biomedical AI pipelines [18, 24, 28, 32, 35,
36]. Our work inherits this recipe, using lightweight vision
encoders, multimodal alignment, and instruction tuning, but
targets volumetric data by feeding compact multi-slice in-
puts to a 2D VLM, preserving architecture while injecting
3D context and keeping compute tractable.
From 2D to 3D Medical Imaging.
Classical volumet-
ric CNNs (e.g., 3D U-Net) and self-configuring pipelines
(nnU-Net) remain powerful segmentation baselines but
are compute/memory intensive at scale [7, 14].
Trans-
former families (UNETR/TransUNet/nnFormer) capture
long-range 3D dependencies and benefit from pretraining
and promptability (Models Genesis, MedSAM) [4, 11, 22,
38, 39]. Language-guided bridges between text and vol-
umes (CT-CLIP) [10] and emerging fully volumetric VLMs
(e.g., Merlin, Med3DVLM) [3, 33] show that end-to-end
3D reasoning is feasible, albeit with higher engineering and
computational costs. A practical challenge is how to re-
tain the benefits of volumetric context (e.g., inter-slice con-
tinuity, spatial coverage) without incurring full 3D model
overhead or overfitting to protocol-specific artifacts. We
therefore pursue a complementary middle path: retain a 2D
VLM backbone and provide just enough volumetric context
through principled slice selection and token concatenation,
aiming for a stronger accuracy–efficiency trade-off than
full-volume modeling while remaining compatible with
instruction-tuned LLM backbones.
Slice Selection for Efficient 3D Modeling. Selecting in-
formative slices offers a pragmatic alternative to processing
entire volumes. Heuristic/optimization strategies such as
energy ranking and k-center prioritize salient views under
limited budgets [12, 16, 23]. In vision–language settings,
selecting diagnostically relevant slices or aggregating se-
quential features improves few/zero-shot performance and
better mirrors radiologist workflows [30]. At the curation
end, non-interactive studies report that, under fixed bud-
gets, annotating fewer slices per volume but more volumes
is typically preferable, and unsupervised active slice selec-
tion may not outperform simple spacing or random sam-
pling [37]. Complementary direction enforces cross-slice
attention for consistency and ordering cues [27]. Concep-
tually, these ideas connect to classic active learning and di-
verse batch acquisition for efficient coverage of informative
examples [2, 9, 15]. Our contribution is to systematize this
line for multi-slice VLMs: we benchmark selection strate-
gies under a unified pipeline, quantify diagnostic accuracy,
explanation quality, and compute, and provide fine-grained
coverage, overlap and error analyses using real-world clini-
cal data.
3. Method
3.1. Adapting 2D Vision–Language Models to
Multi-Slice Medical Inputs
Standard vision-language models (VLMs) are primarily de-
signed for 2D images and cannot directly handle volumetric
data. To address this, we propose a straightforward adapta-
tion that converts 3D images into sequences of 2D slices,
ensuring compatibility with 2D VLM architectures while
preserving essential spatial and anatomical information.
All slices from a patient are first passed through the vi-
sion encoder jointly, yielding patch-level features for each
slice. These features are then grouped by patient and con-
catenated along the sequence dimension to form a single
long token sequence.
