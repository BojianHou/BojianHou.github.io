Abstract
Survival clustering approaches estimate time-to-event pre-
diction and clustering simultaneously.
Current methods,
however, are often limited by their reliance on single data
modalities and their separation of risk prediction from pa-
tient subtyping.
This creates a pressing need for a uni-
fied framework that can simultaneously discover patient
subgroups and predict their conversion risk by integrat-
ing diverse biomarker data.
Here, we introduce Multi-
Modal Deep Clustering Survival Machines (MMDCSM),
a unified framework that (1) encodes each modality via
modality-specific MultiLayer Perceptrons (MLPs), (2) fuses
embeddings into a joint representation, and (3) models sur-
vival outcomes through a mixture of Weibull expert distri-
butions whose soft assignments simultaneously define pa-
tient subtypes and individualized survival curves. Applied
to a cohort of 382 Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) pa-
tients, MMDCSM significantly outperformed existing meth-
ods in identifying distinct low- and high-risk subgroups
for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) conversion while delivering
competitive accuracy in predicting each patient’s personal-
ized timeline to progression. Our model also identified key
brain regions, including the hippocampus, that were most
influential in distinguishing high-risk converters. This ap-
proach enables more accurate, early-stage risk stratifica-
tion, paving the way for targeted interventions designed to
delay or prevent disease progression.
1. Introduction
Early prediction of conversion from Mild Cognitive Impair-
ment (MCI) to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is critical for ef-
fective disease management and early intervention. In the
current clinical practice, the diagnosis of dementia cannot
be made until the patient exhibits clear signs of cognitive
decline. Therefore, methods for predicting the probability
of a patient developing dementia as a function of time, col-
lectively known as survival analysis, are important tools in
helping understanding the characteristics of dementia. Sur-
vival analysis is typically used by medical researchers to
evaluate how explanatory variables influence the time to
an event, such as death, cancer recurrence, or other clini-
cal outcomes that may be censored when only partial in-
formation is available (e.g., due to study withdrawal) [2].
A widely used approach is the Cox proportional hazards
model (Cox PH) [4], which assumes a constant hazard ra-
tio over time for different individuals. More recently, deep
learning techniques have demonstrated comparable or supe-
rior performance to traditional methods in survival predic-
tion (DeepSurv [19], Deep Survival Machines (DSM) [26]).
Clustering, on the other hand, serves as a valuable tool in
data-driven discovery and subtyping of diseases. Clustering
based on risk-profiles in survival analysis (survival cluster-
ing) is critical in applications such as clinical decision mak-
ing, but is relatively under-explored in deep learning. Only
a few methods have been proposed in this context and they
either have limited scalability in high-dimensional, unstruc-
tured data [22], or they focus on the discovery of purely

outcome-driven clusters. For example, we can use survival

for each subject and stratify them using the predicted risk in
a post-hoc way. We can set a threshold (usually the me-
dian or the mean of the whole risk scores) to get the sub-
types. Recently, some models [3, 10, 11, 15, 23] have been
proposed to perform both clustering and time-to-event pre-

(DCSM) [10, 11] combines both discriminative and gener-
ative mechanisms, by assuming that the timing information
can be generatively described by a mixture of expert distri-
butions, then they assign the cluster by the weights learned
from these expert distributions for individual instances in a
discriminative manner.
Although survival-clustering models have shown strong
performance in both subtype discovery and risk predic-
tion, nearly all existing frameworks are restricted to single-
modality data. This limitation is especially problematic for
dementia research, where Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pro-
gression is driven by a constellation of factors—including
structural Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) atrophy pat-
terns, amyloid-β/tau Positron Emission Tomography (PET)
or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) abnormalities, APOE-4 car-
rier status, and cognitive assessments—that physicians rou-
tinely evaluate in combination.
A multimodal survival-
clustering strategy could therefore provide a more nuanced
picture of disease heterogeneity. To date, many machine-
learning pipelines have accurately classified AD using mul-
timodal inputs [18, 21, 34], but they treat diagnosis as a
static outcome and ignore time-to-event information. Con-
versely, recent multimodal survival models predict the prob-
ability of conversion to AD [20, 25] yet do not identify
latent patient clusters.
An extension of Deep Clustering
Survival Machines (DCSM) has begun to address this gap,
demonstrating state-of-the-art clustering and competitive
risk prediction [27]; however, its reliance on a prelimi-
nary feature-selection step risks discarding complementary
cross-modal signals.

each characterized by a distinct risk profile for conversion

• Comprehensive biomarker integration. AD pathology
cannot be captured by any single marker.
MMDCSM
jointly models structural MRI atrophy patterns, PET-

of disease heterogeneity.
• Early-stage precision. Because MCI represents the pro-
dromal phase between normal cognition and AD, strati-
fying MCI patients by individualized conversion risk en-

gression.
• Risk-aware clustering.
Unlike conventional binary
models that merely predict eventual conversion, MMD-
CSM delivers both cluster membership and a personal-
ized survival curve, quantifying how soon each subtype is
likely to progress. In particular, we show that MMDCSM
outperforms baseline methods in terms of both identify-

• Model interpretability.
Post-hoc feature-importance
analysis highlights the brain regions most influential in
distinguishing low- versus high-risk clusters, offering in-
sights that may guide future therapeutic targets for AD.
2. Related Work
Survival analysis estimates the probability that an event of
interest has not yet occurred by time t given a subject’s
covariates X [6]. This probability is expressed by the sur-
vival function S(·|X) = P(T > t|X). Because many studies
end before every subject experiences the event, the data are
typically right-censored. Each record in the dataset D =
{xi,ti,δi}N
i=1 contains a feature vector xi ∈Rd, a follow-up
time ti, and an event indicator δi ∈{0,1} (where δi = 1 indi-
cates that the event was observed at time, and δi = 0 for cen-
sored data). We denote the uncensored and censored subsets
by DU and DC, respectively.
Time-to-event prediction.
Cox proportional hazards
(Cox PH) [4] models the hazard as a multiplicative func-
tion of the covariates and assumes that hazard ratios be-
tween subjects remain constant over time – a restriction
often violated in practice. Deep Cox model (also called
DeepSurv) [19] relaxes Cox PH’s linearity by replacing
the log-risk function with a deep neural network, capturing
complex non-linear relationships while still relying on the
proportional-hazards assumption. Deep Survival Machines
(DSM) [26] discards the proportional-hazards constraint en-
tirely, fitting a mixture of parametric survival distributions
that can model arbitrary, time-varying hazards.
Time-to-event prediction and Clustering.
When used
post-hoc, Cox PH, Deep Cox (DeepSurv), and DSM can
stratify mild-cognitive-impairment (MCI) patients by con-
verting the predicted risk scores into two groups using
the median threshold. Deep Clustering Survival Machines
(DCSM) [11] advances prior work by jointly learning risk
prediction and patient clustering within a unified architec-
ture, but it is limited to single-modal inputs. While an ex-
tension of DCSM has begun to address this limitation [27],
it still relies on a preliminary feature selection step and does
not fully integrate multimodal information during training.
